<p class="tldr"><strong>In short:</strong> law firms that do well on social media answer real client questions in plain English, put their attorneys ahead of the logo, and have a licensed lawyer approve every post before it goes out. LinkedIn comes first for most firms. Facebook and Instagram earn their place for consumer practices like family law, estate planning and personal injury.</p>

My day job is leading marketing and brand at a law firm. I've seen up close how much care legal content needs, and how easily it turns into posts that are technically correct and impossible to read.

Attorneys start with an advantage most businesses would envy: people want what you know. They just want it without the legalese.

## Start with the questions clients already ask

Your best content source is the list of questions your attorneys and intake staff answer every week.

- "Do I need a will if I don't have much money?"
- "What's the difference between a will and a living trust?"
- "How long do I have to file a claim after a car accident?"
- "Do we have to go to court to get divorced?"

Every one of those is a post, and several could fill a series. Ask your team to jot down the questions they hear most for two weeks and you'll have a quarter's worth of ideas.

I built the [Ashby Grant sample case study](../../work/ashbygrant/) around this idea: a weekly "Ask Ashby" post that answers one real question in a fixed format, so followers recognize it before they've read a word.

## Which platforms are worth your time

**LinkedIn** comes first for most firms. Referral sources, in-house counsel, other professionals and future associates all spend time there. It suits business litigation, employment law, corporate work and any practice with a business audience.

**Facebook** is underrated for consumer practices. Local community groups, older clients and the adult children helping parents plan their estates are all there, which makes it a natural fit for estate planning, elder law and family law.

**Instagram** works when you have people and places to show: a friendly team, a downtown office, a family law attorney explaining one thing in under a minute. It's less about legal detail and more about showing who clients will be speaking to.

You don't need all three. One platform done well beats three done halfway.

## What to post (with examples)

**Plain-English explainers.** One question, one answer. If a reader understands it without clicking anything, you've done it right. Carousels suit this well: one step per slide, short sentences, and a fuller explanation in the caption.

**Myth versus fact.** "If I die without a will, everything automatically goes to my spouse." In many states, that isn't how intestacy works. Posts like this get shared because they surprise people. Keep them general, and note that the answer depends on the state.

**Attorney profiles.** Posts from a partner's or associate's own LinkedIn profile usually reach further than posts from the firm page, because readers trust a named person more than a logo. A short first-person post ("the question I now ask every client who comes in to update their will") builds more trust than a stock photo of a gavel.

**Short, subtitled video.** An attorney explaining one thing to camera in under a minute, filmed on a phone, with captions for people watching on mute. Clarity matters more than production value.

**Timely posts.** A new state law taking effect, tax season, the start of the school year for co-parenting schedules, the stretch after the holidays when many people start thinking about divorce. Posts tied to moments people already care about land better than generic ones.

## What to avoid

- **Anything about a current matter**, even with names removed, unless the client has given informed consent and you're sure nobody could identify them.
- **Advice dressed up as information.** General guidance is fine. "Here's what you should do" in reply to someone's comment about their own case is not.
- **Promising outcomes or quoting results without context.** "We'll win your case" has no place in a post, and a big verdict shared without context can mislead.
- **Testimonials and reviews you can't stand behind.** Several states restrict how they can be used.
- **"Specialist" or "expert" claims** unless your state's rules allow them (more on that below).
- **Jargon.** If a client couldn't follow the caption, rewrite it.

## The compliance part, kept simple

I'm not a lawyer, and nothing here is legal advice. Every post should be approved by a licensed attorney at your firm who knows your state's rules. With that said, here's the landscape as I understand it from working in-house.

The American Bar Association's [Model Rules of Professional Conduct](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/model_rules_of_professional_conduct_table_of_contents/) are the template most states build on. Four rules come up again and again for social media:

- **Rule 7.1** bars false or misleading communications about a lawyer's services. According to the [ABA's comment on Rule 7.1](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communication_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/comment_on_rule_7_1/), even a truthful statement can be misleading if it leaves out a fact needed to make the whole message accurate.
- **Rule 7.2** covers specific rules for communications about a lawyer's services, including claims of certification. Under 7.2(c), a lawyer shouldn't say or imply they're a certified specialist unless certified by an organization approved by the state or accredited by the ABA, and that organization is named.
- **Rule 7.3** deals with solicitation. Under the Model Rule, live person-to-person contact (in person, by phone or on a live video call) to seek paid work is generally prohibited. The ABA's version treats written messages differently, but some states are stricter: Pennsylvania, for example, now treats text messages as person-to-person contact. Before anyone at the firm messages a stranger who posted about their accident or divorce, check your state's rule.
- **Rule 1.6** covers confidentiality. In [Formal Opinion 480](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-480.pdf), the ABA said this applies to blogs and social media too, including information that's already in a public court record, and warned against hypotheticals that could let readers identify a client.

Each state bar adopts its own version, and the details differ. Some states want ads filed with the bar, some require particular disclaimers or an "Attorney Advertising" style label, and many restrict testimonials and specialist claims. A few examples:

- **Florida** generally requires lawyers to file advertisements with The Florida Bar before they run. A lawyer's own unpaid social posts are [exempt from filing](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-news/questions-about-digital-advertising/) but still have to follow the substantive rules, and sponsored or boosted posts have to be filed. The Bar's [compliance checklist](https://www.floridabar.org/ethics/etad/quick-reference-checklist-print-internet-sponsored-social-media-not-targeted-tv-radio-ads/) places conditions on testimonials, including a disclaimer that results may differ.
- **Texas** requires many advertisements to be [filed with the State Bar's Advertising Review Committee](https://www.legalethicstexas.com/resources/rules/texas-disciplinary-rules-of-professional-conduct/filing-requirements-for-advertisements-and-solicitation-communications/) unless an exemption applies, and limits certification claims to those recognized by the Texas Board of Legal Specialization or an organization it accredits.
- **New York** overhauled its advertising rules effective June 1, 2026. The [New York State Bar Association's summary](https://nysba.org/understanding-changes-to-new-york-state-lawyer-advertising-rules/) notes that the old "Attorney Advertising" label requirement is gone, while specialist claims are still regulated and testimonials still need the client's permission.

That last example is a good reminder that these rules change. If your posting process was set up a few years ago, it's worth asking your firm's ethics or risk partner to check it against the current rules in every state where your attorneys are licensed.

The simplest safeguard is a named approver. Every post that touches the law gets checked by a licensed attorney before it's scheduled. With a monthly calendar planned in advance, that's one review session, rather than twenty interruptions.

## Keeping it going when you're busy

Law firm social media usually stalls in a busy month. Someone's preparing for trial, posting stops, and three months later the latest post is still the holiday card.

What keeps it going:

1. **A monthly calendar**, planned and approved before the month begins. I walk through how I build one in [how to plan a month of social media content](../social-media-content-plan-small-business/).
2. **Repeatable formats.** A weekly Q&A or a monthly myth-buster means nobody starts from a blank page.
3. **Batching.** Film four short videos in one sitting. Write a month of captions in one afternoon.
4. **Someone who owns it.** Whether that's a member of staff or a freelancer, it can't be "whoever has time," because nobody ever does.
