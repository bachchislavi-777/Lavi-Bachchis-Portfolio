<p class="tldr"><strong>The short answer:</strong> a content creator makes the posts: photos, video, graphics and captions. A social media manager plans what goes out, schedules it, replies to people and reports on it. Many small businesses need a bit of both, so the real question is which gap is bigger: making good content, or keeping it going.</p>

These two roles overlap, and a lot of freelancers do both, which makes the job titles confusing. Here is how I'd tell them apart if I were hiring for my own business.

## What does a content creator do?

A content creator produces the material: photographs, short videos, graphics, carousels and the words that go with them. Their strength is making things people want to look at.

You will see two kinds mentioned:

- **UGC creators** (user-generated content) are paid to make content in the style of a real customer. The brand then uses it on its own channels or in its ads.
- **Influencers** post on their own channels to their own audience, usually for a fee or free product.

They are different jobs with different rules, which I cover below.

## What does a social media manager do?

A manager looks after the account as a whole. In a typical month that means a content calendar you approve, scheduling every post, replying to comments and messages if that is included, and a short report on what worked. They may make the content as well, or they may work with what you give them.

## What's the difference between a manager and a creator?

| | Content creator | Social media manager |
|---|---|---|
| Main output | Photos, video, graphics, captions | A planned calendar, scheduled posts, replies, reporting |
| Key skills | Making things look and sound good | Planning, consistency, community, analysis |
| Time commitment | Usually project or batch based | Ongoing, every week |
| Typical pricing | Per piece or bundle | Often a fixed monthly fee |
| Best for | A business short of good material | A business short of time or consistency |

I haven't put figures in the table, because they vary too much between people and projects. For ballpark ranges on the management side, see my guide to [what a freelance social media manager costs in the UK](../freelance-social-media-manager-cost-uk/).

## Which do you need first?

Answer honestly:

- **Your photos and graphics look amateur, but you post fairly often?** Start with a creator.
- **You have decent material, but posting stops whenever you get busy?** Start with a manager.
- **Nobody replies to comments and messages?** A manager.
- **You want fresh product shots or a library of short videos?** A creator.
- **You are launching a product?** Probably a creator first, then a manager to keep it running.
- **You have a tight budget?** Fix the bigger problem first. A good batch of content, scheduled by you, can carry a quiet month.

If you're still unsure, a [simple monthly content plan](../social-media-content-plan-small-business/) will show you which gap is biggest within a few weeks.

## What should you agree with a content creator?

Put these in writing before anything is made:

1. **Usage rights.** Where can you use the content (your feed, your website, ads), and for how long?
2. **Revisions.** How many rounds of changes are included?
3. **Raw files.** Do you receive the originals, or just the finished edit?
4. **Ownership.** In UK law the creator is usually the first owner of what they make, and an assignment has to be in writing. So ask what you are getting: ownership, or a licence to use it.
5. **Labelling.** This is the one people trip over.

If you pay an influencer to post about you on their channel, it is an ad and has to be labelled as one. The ASA's [guidance on recognising ads](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recognising-ads-social-media.html) says the label should be prominent and clear, usually at the start of the post, and that words like "gifted" or "in association with" aren't enough on their own. Both the brand and the influencer share responsibility for getting it right.

Reviews are a separate issue. Since 6 April 2025 the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 has banned fake reviews, which includes reviews that were paid for or incentivised without that being made clear. The CMA's [guidance on the Act's unfair trading rules](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691b9bd821ef5aaa6543ee6f/Unfair_commercial_practices_CMA207_18_Nov_2025__2_.pdf) is the place to check the detail. In practice: don't post a paid-for video as if it were an ordinary customer's honest review. This is general information, not legal advice.

## What mistakes do small businesses make when hiring for this?

The ones I see most often:

- **Hiring a creator and expecting a plan.** Lovely content with no calendar behind it tends to appear in bursts and then stop.
- **Hiring a manager and supplying nothing.** If you expect a manager to make everything from scratch, check the quote includes design and writing, because some don't.
- **Not saying who approves.** A named person on your side, who can reply within a day or two, keeps the month moving.
- **Paying for lots of content and posting little of it.** A smaller amount of good work, shared regularly, usually beats a big batch that sits unused.

Ask each person to describe a typical month, step by step. The answer tells you which role they really fill.
