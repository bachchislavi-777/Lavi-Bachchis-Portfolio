<p class="tldr"><strong>The short answer:</strong> a content creator makes the posts: photos, video, graphics and captions. A social media manager plans what goes out, schedules it, replies to people and reports on it. Many small businesses need a bit of both, so the real question is which gap is bigger: making good content, or keeping it going.</p>

The two titles get used interchangeably, and that causes expensive mismatches. Hire a creator when what you needed was someone to run the account, and you end up with lovely videos that nobody schedules or answers comments on. Here's how to tell which gap you have.

## What does a content creator do?

A content creator produces the material: photos, video, graphics and the captions that go with them. The term covers a few different jobs:

- **A freelance content creator** makes assets for you, often as a bundle or per piece.
- **A UGC creator** makes content in the style of a real customer. You pay for it and use it on your own channels and in ads.
- **An influencer** posts about you on their own channels, to their own audience.

The last one is a different arrangement altogether, with different rules, which I cover below.

## What does a social media manager do?

A manager runs the account as a whole. That includes a monthly plan, designing or briefing the content, writing captions, scheduling, replying to comments and messages, and a short report. You're paying for the system around the content more than any single post.

## What's the difference between a manager and a creator?

A creator's job ends when the asset is delivered; a manager's job continues after it's posted, which is why the two are priced and scoped so differently. This comparison makes it concrete:

| | Content creator | Social media manager |
|---|---|---|
| Main output | Photos, video, graphics, captions | A plan, a posting schedule, replies, reporting |
| Key skill | Making things people stop for | Keeping a consistent, on-brand presence |
| Time commitment | Project or per-piece | Ongoing, every month |
| Usual pricing | Per piece or per bundle | Monthly retainer |
| Typical risk | Great assets that never get used | Steady posting with flat content |

For how managers charge, see [freelance social media manager rates and cost](../freelance-social-media-manager-cost-us/). Creator rates vary widely with format, usage rights and experience, and I haven't found a reliable public benchmark, so get quotes for a specific brief.

## Which should you hire first?

Pick the role that fixes your biggest bottleneck:

- **You have no usable photos or video:** a content creator first. A roofing company with no project photos needs assets before it needs a schedule.
- **You have content sitting in a camera roll and nothing goes out:** a manager first. A food truck owner who shoots great clips but posts twice a month is a typical case.
- **You have neither time nor material:** look for one person who can plan and produce, or phase it: creator first, manager next.
- **You sell products with a strong visual look:** an Etsy jewelry shop may want a creator for hero shots, then a manager to keep the feed consistent.
- **You're tight on budget:** commission a small creator bundle, then plan a month yourself using a [content calendar](../social-media-content-plan-small-business/).

## How do content creators charge?

Most creators price per piece or per bundle rather than monthly. The price usually reflects four things: the format (a still photo costs less to produce than an edited video), the number of revisions, how long you can use the content, and whether you can run it in paid ads. A creator who hands over raw files will often charge more than one who delivers only the finished edit. Always compare like with like: ask each creator to quote against the same written brief.

## What should a good creator brief include?

A clear brief saves everybody time and cuts down on revisions. Include:

- **The goal of the content.** A product launch, a recurring series, or a bank of photos for your feed.
- **Brand basics.** Your colors, tone of voice and two or three examples you like.
- **Formats and sizes.** For example vertical video for Reels, or square photos for the grid.
- **Deadlines and approval steps.** Who signs off, and by when.
- **Do-nots.** Claims you can't make, competitors you don't want mentioned, anything that needs sign-off.

## What should you agree on with a content creator?

Agree on the usage rights in writing before anyone shoots a frame. Four points matter most:

1. **Usage rights.** Where can you use the content, for how long, and can you run it as a paid ad? Work by a freelancer generally belongs to the freelancer unless a signed agreement transfers or licenses it, so get this on paper.
2. **Revisions.** How many rounds of changes are included?
3. **Raw files.** Do you receive the originals or only the finished edit?
4. **Disclosure.** If a creator posts about you on their own channel because you paid them or sent free products, the FTC expects a clear disclosure. Its [Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers) says to put it with the post itself and use plain words like "ad" or "sponsored."

You may hear the term "whitelisting," which means a brand runs ads through a creator's handle. That needs explicit permission and a clear agreement on how long the ads run.

Reviews have their own rules. The FTC's [Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers), effective October 21, 2024, prohibits fake or false reviews and undisclosed insider reviews, and buying fake social media indicators such as bot followers. Paid creators are typically independent contractors, so the usual paperwork applies; see [how to hire a freelance social media manager](../how-to-hire-a-freelance-social-media-manager-us/). This isn't legal advice, so check with an attorney if you're unsure.

## Can one person do both?

Often, yes, within limits. When I work on a month of content, I design and write the posts and also plan and schedule them, so one person is briefed once and nothing is lost in a hand-off. I edit short-form video for Reels, but I don't film on location, so a business that needs a photographer or a video shoot should budget for that separately.
