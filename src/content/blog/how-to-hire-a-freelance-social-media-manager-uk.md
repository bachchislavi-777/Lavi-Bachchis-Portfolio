<p class="tldr"><strong>The short answer:</strong> decide what you need done (posting, content creation or strategy), shortlist two or three freelancers whose work looks like the standard you want, ask each the same questions, and get a written agreement before anyone touches your accounts. It should cover deliverables, approvals, notice, who owns the files and how they get access.</p>

Hiring someone to speak for your business in public is a bigger decision than it first looks. I've sat on the freelancer's side of a lot of first calls, so here is what I'd want to know if I were the one doing the hiring.

## What should you decide before you start looking?

Write down the job before you write the shortlist, because "social media management" means different things to different people. A quick checklist:

- **Platforms.** Which one or two matter to your customers? Be realistic.
- **Volume.** How many posts a week, and in what formats (single images, carousels, Reels)?
- **Who supplies what.** Photos, copy, design, video: who makes each?
- **Community management.** Who replies to comments and messages, and how fast?
- **Extras.** Newsletters, blog posts, reporting?
- **Approval.** Who on your side signs things off, and how quickly?

Once you have that, your budget is easier to set. My guide to [what a freelance social media manager costs in the UK](../freelance-social-media-manager-cost-uk/) has the ballpark ranges.

## Where can you find a freelance social media manager in the UK?

The usual routes, each with a catch:

- **Recommendations.** Ask other business owners in your area or sector. Slow, but usually the most reliable.
- **LinkedIn.** Search the title and look at what people actually post, not just their headline.
- **Portfolio websites.** Freelancers with their own site have chosen to show their work properly, which tells you something.
- **Marketplaces.** Fast, with lots of choice. They also tend to be price-led and mixed in quality, so you still need to vet each person.

You don't need someone in your town, or in London. Most of this work is done remotely, though some businesses like to meet in person at the start, and that's a perfectly good thing to ask for.

## How do you judge a portfolio?

Look at the whole grid, not the best single post. Does the feed look consistent? Do the captions sound like a real brand rather than a template? Has the freelancer worked in anything close to your sector?

Check that samples are labelled honestly. My own portfolio is made up of sample brands that I created to show how I work, and each one says so. A freelancer who is upfront about what is sample work and what isn't is easier to trust. ## What should you ask on the first call?

Ask every candidate the same questions, so you can compare answers fairly:

1. What exactly is included in the monthly fee?
2. Who designs, and who writes the captions?
3. How many rounds of changes are included per piece?
4. How do approvals work, and does anything go live without my sign-off?
5. Who replies to comments and DMs?
6. What do you report on, and how often?
7. Which tools do you use, and who pays for them?
8. What is the notice period?
9. Who owns the files and templates if we stop working together?
10. How will you access my accounts?

Vague answers to the last four are the ones I'd worry about most.

## What should the agreement cover?

A short, plain agreement is enough for most small jobs. Make sure it includes:

| Clause | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| Deliverables | A clear monthly list: platforms, number of posts, formats, newsletters if any |
| Fee and VAT | A fixed monthly fee, with it stated whether VAT is included |
| Revisions | A set number of rounds per piece (two is common) |
| Approval | Nothing is published without your sign-off |
| Notice | Rolling monthly with around 30 days' notice, not a long lock-in |
| Ownership | In UK law the person who creates a work is usually its first owner, and an assignment of copyright has to be in writing and signed. So ask for ownership or a licence to pass to you on payment |
| Account access | Added as a user, never sharing your passwords |
| Confidentiality and data | What they can see, and what they must keep private |

On ownership, the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 is the source: [section 11](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/11) covers the first owner and [section 90](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/90) covers assignment. Neither is a reason to panic, just a reason to put it in writing.

## Do IR35 and employment status apply?

Usually not in a way that causes small businesses much trouble, but it's worth understanding. IR35, also called the off-payroll working rules, applies when someone provides services through an intermediary, most often their own limited company. HMRC's [guidance on off-payroll working](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/understanding-off-payroll-working-ir35) explains that when the client is a small business outside the public sector, it's the worker's own intermediary that decides whether the rules apply. The rules are written for people who work through an intermediary. If a freelancer invoices you directly as a sole trader, the practical question is simply whether they are genuinely self-employed.

What does point towards genuine self-employment? A defined monthly deliverable rather than "whatever I ask for", the freelancer using their own tools and setting their own hours, and being free to work for other clients. [GOV.UK's guidance for self-employed contractors](https://www.gov.uk/employment-status/selfemployed-contractor) sets out the typical signs, and HMRC's Check Employment Status for Tax (CEST) tool is there if you want a second opinion.

This isn't tax advice, and each arrangement depends on its facts, so check with your accountant if you're unsure.

### What about VAT?

Ask whether the quote includes VAT. A freelancer who is VAT-registered adds 20%, and registration is compulsory once taxable turnover goes above [£90,000](https://www.gov.uk/vat-registration-thresholds), the figure at the time of writing. Many small freelancers are under it, but asking means you compare like with like.

## How do you keep control of your accounts and data?

Never share a password. Add the freelancer as a user on Meta Business Suite and give them an appropriate role on your LinkedIn page. You stay the owner, and ending the arrangement is a two-minute job.

If they'll see customer information, such as direct messages or a Mailchimp list, they're handling personal data for you. Under UK GDPR, when someone processes personal data on your behalf, you need a written contract in place with them. The ICO explains this in its [guidance on contracts](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/accountability-and-governance/guide-to-accountability-and-governance/contracts/). A data clause in your agreement usually covers it, and email marketing also has to follow PECR, so make sure everyone on your newsletter list has given proper permission.

## What does a good first month look like?

Here's how I scope a new month, so you know what normal looks like:

1. A short intro call to understand the business.
2. A brand kit, so colours, fonts and tone are set once.
3. A monthly calendar that you approve before anything is made.
4. Design, writing and scheduling, with two rounds of changes per piece.
5. Templates built in your own Canva account, so they stay yours.

I usually start within about two weeks of agreeing the plan.

One more rule to know: your posts count as advertising under the ASA's CAP Code, so claims need to be honest. If you pay a creator to feature your product, the post has to be clearly labelled as an ad, such as "Ad" at the start, as the [ASA explains](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recognising-ads-social-media.html).

No honest freelancer can promise followers, leads or sales, because those depend on your offer, prices, market and budget. Be wary of anyone who does.
