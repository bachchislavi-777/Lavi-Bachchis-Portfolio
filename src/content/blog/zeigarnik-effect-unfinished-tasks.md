<p class="tldr"><strong>In short:</strong> the Zeigarnik effect is our tendency to remember unfinished tasks better than finished ones. An incomplete task stays "open" in your mind and keeps nudging you. The good news is you don't always have to finish something to quiet it: making a clear plan for when you'll do it can be enough.</p>

Have you ever had a productive day, ticked off ten things, and still gone to bed feeling like you didn't do enough?

It's usually because of the one thing you didn't finish. That single unanswered email or half-written report somehow outweighs everything you did get done. It follows you into the evening, pops up while you're brushing your teeth and sits in the back of your mind like a sticky note you can't peel off.

It has a name: the Zeigarnik effect.

## Where the Zeigarnik effect comes from

The effect is named after Bluma Zeigarnik, a psychologist who studied it in Berlin in the 1920s, working with Kurt Lewin.

The story often told is that Lewin noticed something about waiters in a café: they could remember long, complicated orders perfectly, right up until the bill was paid. Then the details seemed to vanish.

Zeigarnik tested the idea. She gave people a series of simple tasks, like puzzles and small handicrafts, and let them finish some but interrupted them partway through others. Afterwards, people tended to remember the interrupted tasks more readily than the ones they'd completed. Her research was published in 1927.

## Why your brain holds on to unfinished tasks

The simplest way to think about it is this: when you start something, your mind opens a loop. That loop stays open, and keeps a little bit of your attention, until the task is done.

That's actually helpful most of the time. It's how you remember to go back to the thing you were doing before the phone rang. Without it, half-finished jobs would simply disappear from your mind.

The trouble is that modern life gives us a lot of open loops at once. Work tasks, messages, life admin, plans with friends, things we said we'd do "next week". Each one keeps a small claim on your attention, and together they can leave you feeling scattered, restless and tired, even when you've done plenty. If that sounds familiar, you might also find it useful to read about [mental exhaustion, and why you can feel tired even after sleeping](../tired-after-sleeping-mental-exhaustion/).

## What the research says now

Psychology research has moved on since 1927.

Since Zeigarnik's original study, many researchers have tried to repeat it, with mixed results. The memory advantage for unfinished tasks doesn't show up for everyone in every situation. It seems to depend on things like how much you care about the task and why it was interrupted.

But the bigger idea, that unfinished goals keep tugging at our thoughts, holds up well. And one study in particular is useful in everyday life.

In 2011, psychologists E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister published a study with a hopeful finding. People who were reminded of an important unfinished goal had more of those goal-related thoughts popping up during other tasks. But when people made a specific plan for how and when they'd complete the goal, those intrusive thoughts dropped away, even though they still hadn't done the task.

In other words, **you don't always have to finish something to let it go. You just have to decide what happens next.**

## How to use the Zeigarnik effect in everyday life

### 1. Close the day with a plan, not a finished list

Before you stop work, write down anything still unfinished and, next to each one, exactly when you'll pick it up. "Reply to Sam: Tuesday, 9am." It sounds almost too simple, but it gives your brain permission to stop holding on.

### 2. Do a "brain dump" before bed

If your mind starts racing at night, keep a notebook by your bed. Write every open loop down, without trying to solve anything. Getting it onto paper tells your brain it's been captured somewhere safe.

### 3. Use it to beat procrastination

The effect works in your favour here. The hardest part of a task you're avoiding is often starting it. Once you've started, even for five minutes, you've opened a loop, and your mind will nudge you to come back and finish it.

### 4. Keep fewer loops open at once

Every half-started job takes a little attention. Where you can, finish small tasks straight away rather than leaving them for later, and limit how many bigger projects you have on the go at the same time.

### 5. Be kind about the one thing you didn't do

At the end of a long day, try listing what you did finish, not just what's left. It won't switch the effect off, but it can help you see the day more fairly.

## A gentle reminder

Feeling like you haven't done enough isn't always a sign that you haven't. Sometimes it's just your brain doing its job a little too well, holding on to the one thing that's still open.

Write it down. Give it a time. Then let yourself rest.
