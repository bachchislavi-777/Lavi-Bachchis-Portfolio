<p class="tldr"><strong>In short:</strong> the law firms that do well on social media answer real client questions in plain English, put their people in front of the logo, and get every post checked before it goes out. LinkedIn comes first for most firms. Instagram and Facebook earn their place for private client, family and residential property work.</p>

My day job is running marketing and brand for a law firm in London. So I've seen, up close, how much care legal content needs, and how easily it slides into posts that are technically correct and completely unreadable.

The good news is that solicitors have something most businesses would love: people genuinely want what you know. They just want it without the Latin.

## Start with the questions clients already ask

The best source of content for a law firm isn't a trends report. It's the questions your fee earners answer on the phone every week.

- "Do I need a will if I'm not married?"
- "What actually happens between exchange and completion?"
- "Who gets the house if we split up and we're not married?"
- "What's a lasting power of attorney, and when do we need one?"

Each of those is a post. Several of them are a whole series. Ask your team to jot down the questions they hear most for a fortnight and you'll have a quarter's worth of ideas.

## Which platforms are worth your time

**LinkedIn** is the obvious one, and for good reason. It's where referrers, in-house counsel, other professionals and your future trainees spend their time. It suits commercial teams, employment law and anything with a business audience.

**Facebook** is underrated for high-street firms. Local groups, older clients and the children helping them make decisions about wills and care are all there.

**Instagram** works when you have people and places to show: a friendly team, an office on the high street, a property team celebrating a busy completion day. It's less about legal detail and more about showing who clients will actually be speaking to.

You don't need all three. Doing one platform properly beats three half-heartedly.

## What to post (with examples)

**Plain-English explainers.** One question, one answer. If someone can understand it without clicking anything, you've done it right. Carousels are perfect for this: one step per slide, short sentences, with the full explanation in the post text.

**Myth versus fact.** "Living together for years makes us common-law spouses." It doesn't, in England and Wales. These posts get shared because people are genuinely surprised.

**Your people.** Posts from a partner's or associate's own LinkedIn profile usually reach further than posts from the firm's page, because people connect with people. A short first-person post about the work ("the question I now ask every client making a will") does more for trust than a stock photo of a gavel.

**Short, subtitled video.** A solicitor explaining one thing to camera for under a minute, filmed on a phone, with subtitles for people watching on mute. It doesn't need to be polished. It needs to be clear.

**Timely posts.** Changes to the law, a tax-year deadline, the January spike in divorce enquiries. Posts tied to moments people already care about land better than generic ones.

## What to avoid

- **Anything about a live matter**, even anonymised, unless the client has agreed and you're sure nobody could identify them.
- **Advice dressed up as information.** General guidance is fine. "Here's what you should do in your situation" in the comments is not.
- **Promising outcomes.** "We'll win your case" has no place in a post.
- **Testimonials you can't stand behind.** If you share client feedback, it should be genuine and fairly represent what clients say.
- **Jargon.** If your mum wouldn't understand the caption, rewrite it.

## The compliance bit, kept simple

I'm not a solicitor, and none of this is legal advice. But here is how I'd approach it in practice.

Posts about the firm count as publicity, so they need to be accurate and not misleading under the SRA's rules. Confidentiality applies online exactly as it does in the office. And the Law Society's [practice note on social media](https://www.lawsociety.org.uk/topics/business-management/social-media) is worth half an hour of anyone's time, including whoever runs your accounts.

The simplest safeguard is a named approver. Every post that touches the law is checked by a qualified person before it's scheduled. It sounds slow, but with a monthly calendar planned in advance, it's one review session, not twenty interruptions.

## Keeping it going when you're busy

Law firm social media usually dies in a busy month. Someone's on a big completion, the posting stops, and three months later the last post is still the Christmas card.

What fixes it:

1. **A monthly calendar**, planned and approved before the month starts. I walk through how I build one in [how to plan a month of social media content](../social-media-content-plan-small-business/).
2. **Repeatable formats.** A weekly Q&A or a monthly myth-buster means you're not starting from a blank page.
3. **Batching.** Film four short videos in one sitting. Write a month of captions in one afternoon.
4. **Someone whose job it is.** Whether that's a member of staff or a freelancer, it can't be "whoever has time", because nobody ever does.
