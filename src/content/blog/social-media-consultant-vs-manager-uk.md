<p class="tldr"><strong>The short answer:</strong> a social media consultant reviews what you are doing and gives you a strategy and plan to run yourself, usually as a one-off project or a few sessions. A social media manager does the work every month: planning, creating, posting and replying. If you have the time but no plan, start with a consultant. If you have a plan but no time, hire a manager.</p>

The two job titles get used interchangeably, which is why so many people end up paying for the wrong one. I do a bit of both, so I'll try to give you the plain version of how they differ.

## What does a freelance social media consultant do?

A consultant helps you decide what to do. The usual pieces are:

- A review of your current accounts and what is and isn't working
- Who your audience is and how you want to sound to them
- Three or four content pillars
- Which platforms deserve your time, and which don't
- A plan for the next 90 days
- Sometimes, training for you or your team

What you get is advice and a document, not posts. You or someone on your team has to do the doing.

## What does a social media manager do?

A manager runs it. In a normal month that means a content calendar you approve, the design and captions for each post, scheduling, replying to comments and messages (if that is included), and a short report at the end.

## Consultant vs manager: what's the difference?

| | Consultant | Manager |
|---|---|---|
| What you get | A strategy, audit and plan | Finished posts, published on schedule |
| Who does the work | You or your team | The freelancer |
| Typical engagement | One-off project or a few sessions | Ongoing, rolling monthly |
| Common pricing | Day rate, fixed project fee or paid sessions | Fixed monthly fee |
| Best for | Businesses with time but no direction | Businesses with direction but no time |
| What you still do | All the creating and posting | Approve the calendar and answer anything only you can |

## How much does a social media consultant charge in the UK?

It depends on the person and the scope, so be wary of anyone who gives you a flat number without asking questions. The pricing models you will meet are:

- **Day rate.** Common in the UK for strategy work.
- **Fixed project fee.** A defined deliverable, such as an audit and 90-day plan, for one price.
- **A few paid sessions.** Useful if you want guidance but intend to do the work.
- **A small monthly retainer.** For regular check-ins as you go.

As a rough guide, consultants tend to sit at the senior end of the freelance range. In my [guide to what freelancers cost](../freelance-social-media-manager-cost-uk/), senior strategists are ballpark above £75 an hour, which is a rough indication rather than a price list. A fixed project fee is usually the easiest to budget for, because you know the total before you start. Whatever you are quoted, ask for it in writing, with exactly what you will receive.

## Which one does your business need?

Work through these:

- **No plan, but time to do it yourself?** Hire a consultant.
- **A plan, but no time?** Hire a manager.
- **Launching or rebranding?** Strategy first, then a manager to run it.
- **In a regulated sector (accountancy, financial services)?** Either can work, but build an approval step into the plan, so a qualified person checks posts before they go live.
- **A very small budget?** One consultant session plus a simple [content plan you run yourself](../social-media-content-plan-small-business/) can go a long way.

For example, an independent café in Bristol with a keen owner and a quiet Tuesday might get more from a single strategy session than from a monthly retainer. A busy accountancy practice that knows what it wants to say but never has the hours would probably do better with a manager.

## Can one freelancer do both?

Often, yes. When I start with a new business, strategy is built into the first month: I put together a brand kit, agree the content pillars and build the calendar you approve before anything is made. That is usually enough for a small business.

If you want a stand-alone piece, such as a review of your accounts or a plan to take away and use yourself, I treat it as a custom project with a fixed quote after a short call, not a package with a name. ## What should a consultant's plan include?

Whoever you hire, a good plan should have:

1. **Goals you can measure,** such as enquiries or newsletter sign-ups, rather than "go viral".
2. **Your audience,** described as real people, not "everyone".
3. **Three or four content pillars.**
4. **Platforms and frequency** you can actually keep up.
5. **Formats,** such as carousels, short video or stories.
6. **A sample month,** so you can see it in practice.
7. **An approval process,** including who signs off.
8. **What to measure** and when to review it.
9. **Rules for paid collaborations.** If you ever pay a creator to feature you, the post needs to be clearly labelled as an ad under the ASA's CAP Code.

If you are handed a long deck with none of the above, ask for the short version.

## What should you watch out for when hiring a consultant?

A few things make me cautious, whoever is pitching:

- **A plan that is mostly generic.** If it could be pasted into any business, it isn't a plan for yours.
- **Guaranteed results.** Nobody controls your market, your prices or the algorithm. An honest consultant will tell you what they can deliver, which is advice and a clear plan, not an outcome.
- **No sample month.** A strategy you cannot picture being carried out will sit in a folder.
- **A plan you can't afford to run.** Ten posts a week sounds impressive until you realise nobody has the hours. The most useful advice is the version you will actually keep up.

It also helps to ask what happens after the handover. Some consultants include a follow-up call a few weeks later. Others stop at the document. Neither is wrong, but you should know which you are buying.
