<p class="tldr"><strong>The short answer:</strong> define what you need first (posting, content creation or strategy), shortlist two or three freelancers whose portfolios match the standard you want, ask each the same questions, and sign a written agreement covering deliverables, approvals, cancellation, ownership of the work and account access. Collect a Form W-9 before the first payment.</p>

Hiring a freelancer for your social media feels risky because you're handing someone your brand's public voice. The good news is that a handful of decisions made up front, plus one page of agreed terms, removes most of the risk. Here's the process I'd follow if I were hiring, and the one I use when I scope a new engagement myself.

## What should you define before you start looking?

Write down what you need done, because "help with social media" produces vague quotes. A short checklist:

- **Platforms.** Which ones, and are you willing to drop one that isn't earning its place?
- **Frequency.** Posts per week on each platform.
- **Content.** Who supplies the photos and video? Who designs? Who writes captions?
- **Community.** Do you want someone replying to comments and DMs, or only publishing?
- **Extras.** Email newsletters, blog posts, reporting.
- **Approvals.** Who on your side signs off, and how fast can they turn things around?

A dental office with one front-desk reviewer, a landscaping company with seasonal peaks and a boutique on Main Street with a weekly drop each need a different brief. For budgeting, see my guide to [freelance social media manager rates and cost](../freelance-social-media-manager-cost-us/).

## Where do US businesses find freelance social media managers?

The most common routes are referrals, LinkedIn, freelancers' own portfolio websites and freelance marketplaces. Referrals from other owners in your area or industry tend to be the most reliable, because you can ask how the working relationship actually went. Nobody can promise a perfect match, so shortlist two or three people and compare.

### Is Upwork a good place to hire a social media manager?

It can be. Marketplaces are quick and give you a payment process and a record of past work in one place. The trade-off is volume: a large pool of bids, many priced low, and a lot of sorting for you to do. The vetting steps below apply wherever you find someone. Check the platform's own fee and payment terms before you commit, because they change.

You don't need a freelancer in your city. Most of this work happens remotely. I'm based in London and work remotely with US small businesses, and drafts land at the start of your business day.

## How do you vet a portfolio?

Look for consistency, voice and fit rather than a single impressive post. Scroll a whole grid, not the highlights.

- **Does the feed look like one brand?** Colors, type and layouts should feel related from post to post.
- **Do the captions sound like a person?** Or like they could belong to any company?
- **Is there sector fit?** Someone who has handled a restaurant's feed will adapt quickly to a food truck.
- **Is the work labeled honestly?** Concept or sample work should be called that. My own portfolio is sample brands I made to show how I work, and each is labeled as sample work. The [Loom House sample](../../work/loomhouse/) shows a brand kit, a planned grid and carousel formats for a made-up homeware brand.

## What questions should you ask on the first call?

Ask every candidate the same list so you can compare answers side by side:

1. What exactly is included each month, and what costs extra?
2. Who designs, and who writes the captions?
3. How many rounds of changes are included?
4. How do approvals work, and what happens if I'm slow to respond?
5. Who replies to comments and messages, and how fast?
6. What does reporting look like, and how often?
7. Which tools do you use, and who pays for them?
8. What time zone do you work in, and when do drafts arrive?
9. What's the notice period if either of us wants to stop?
10. Who owns the files and templates, and how will you access my accounts?

Listen for specific answers. "It depends" is fair once, but a freelancer who can't describe their process is telling you something.

## What should a social media management contract include?

A simple agreement is enough, as long as it's in writing. Cover these clauses:

| Clause | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| Scope | Monthly deliverables listed by number and type (for example, 12 posts, 2 Reels, 1 newsletter) |
| Fee and payment | A fixed fee, due date, and what's billed separately (ad spend, paid tools) |
| Revisions | A stated number of rounds per piece |
| Approvals | You approve the calendar and each piece before anything goes live |
| Term and cancellation | Month-to-month after a first month, with about 30 days' notice |
| Ownership | A signed written assignment or license of the finished work to you, effective on payment |
| Account access | Admin or user roles on accounts you own; no shared personal logins |
| Confidentiality | Neither side shares the other's private information |

Ownership deserves a moment. Under U.S. copyright law, work by an independent contractor generally belongs to the contractor unless it qualifies as a "work made for hire" or the contractor signs a written transfer. The [Copyright Office explains](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf) that a commissioned work counts as work made for hire only if it falls into specific statutory categories and both sides sign a written agreement saying so. Social posts often don't fit those categories, so ask for a written assignment (or at least a clear license) instead of assuming you own what you paid for. For wording, talk to an attorney.

## Is a freelance social media manager a 1099 contractor?

Usually, yes: most freelancers are hired as independent contractors rather than employees. Ask for a Form W-9 before you pay anyone, so you have their legal name, address and taxpayer ID on file.

The IRS says to report payments to a non-employee for services in the course of your business when they total [$2,000 or more in a calendar year for payments made after December 31, 2025](https://www.irs.gov/faqs/small-business-self-employed-other-business/form-1099-nec-independent-contractors/form-1099-nec-independent-contractors) (it was $600 before that). Form 1099-NEC is the usual form. Exceptions apply: for instance, payments made by credit card or through a third-party payment network are generally reported by the payment processor on Form 1099-K, not by you, according to the [IRS instructions for Form 1099-NEC](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099mec). The IRS says the threshold will be adjusted for inflation after 2026, so check [the current figure](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/am-i-required-to-file-a-form-1099-or-other-information-return) each year or ask your CPA.

Whether someone is truly a contractor depends on the relationship, not the label. The [IRS looks at](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee) behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship. A freelancer who sets their own hours, uses their own tools and works for other clients looks different from someone you direct day to day. Some states apply stricter tests; California's "ABC test" is the best-known example. This isn't tax or legal advice, so confirm with your CPA or an attorney.

## How do you protect your accounts and data?

Keep every account in your name and give the freelancer access as a user. Use business-level roles, such as Meta Business Suite and a LinkedIn page admin role, and never share a personal login. If the relationship ends, you remove access in one step and keep your followers, your content history and your ad accounts.

If your freelancer will send newsletters, the CAN-SPAM Act applies to commercial email. The FTC's [compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) says each message needs a clear way to opt out, a valid physical postal address, and opt-outs honored within 10 business days. Penalties can reach tens of thousands of dollars per email, so confirm your freelancer builds this in.

## What advertising rules apply to your posts?

The FTC expects clear disclosure whenever there's a "material connection" between a brand and someone endorsing it, including free products. Its [Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers) says the disclosure should be hard to miss and placed with the endorsement itself, using plain words such as "ad" or "sponsored."

The FTC's [Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers), effective October 21, 2024, also prohibits fake reviews and testimonials, undisclosed insider reviews, and buying fake indicators of social influence such as bot followers. Don't hire anyone who offers to buy followers or reviews. For anything beyond the basics, ask an attorney.
