<p class="tldr"><strong>The short answer:</strong> most UK small businesses pay a freelance social media manager a fixed monthly fee of roughly £250 to £1,200. Where you land depends on how many platforms you're on, how many posts you need, and whether design and copywriting are both included. Ad spend is almost always on top of that.</p>

I get asked this on almost every intro call, usually a bit awkwardly, as if it's rude to bring up money. It isn't. It's the most sensible question you can ask, so here's the straight version, including the bits people selling social media packages don't always volunteer. I'm based in London and work remotely with small businesses across the UK.

## How much does a freelance social media manager charge per month in the UK?

Prices vary a lot, and anyone who gives you one number is guessing. But when you collect a few quotes in the UK, they tend to fall into three rough bands:

| What you're buying | Typical monthly fee | What it usually looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Scheduling and light posting | £250 to £500 | Posting content you mostly supply, on one or two platforms |
| Done-for-you content | £500 to £1,200 | Planning, design and captions on two or three platforms |
| Content plus extras | £1,200+ | Adds newsletters, blogs, video editing, reporting or community management |

These are ballpark figures, based on quotes and rate cards I've seen in 2025 and 2026, not a price list. A skincare brand that needs four beautifully designed posts a week is a very different job from a plumber who wants one helpful tip every Tuesday.

## What is the hourly rate for a freelance social media manager in the UK?

Hourly rates are often quoted somewhere between £20 and £75 an hour, though that's a ballpark, not a rule. Very junior help is usually below that, and senior strategists are above it. Most freelancers who run accounts month to month charge a fixed fee instead, because it's easier to compare quotes and nobody gets a surprise invoice.

| Level | Ballpark hourly rate |
|---|---|
| Junior or just starting out | Below £20 |
| Experienced freelancer | £20 to £75 |
| Senior strategist | Above £75 |

If you need someone to set the strategy rather than run the accounts every month, that's a consultant's job, and it's priced differently. See [social media consultant or manager?](../social-media-consultant-vs-manager-uk/) for how the two compare.

## Why do two quotes for social media management differ so much?

Because they're often not quoting for the same thing. "Social media management" can mean anything from "I'll press publish on posts you send me" to "I'll plan your month, design every graphic, write every caption and reply to your comments." That's how you end up with two quotes £800 apart.

The things that move the price most:

- **Who makes the content.** If you're supplying photos and copy, it's cheaper. If the freelancer designs and writes everything, you're paying for two skills.
- **Number of platforms.** Instagram and LinkedIn need different formats and a different tone. Adding a platform isn't copy and paste.
- **Video.** Reels and short videos take far longer than a static post. Check whether they're included or priced per video.
- **Community management.** Replying to comments and DMs every day is a real time commitment, and many packages leave it out.
- **Reporting.** A monthly report is useful. A 20-page deck nobody reads is not.
- **Your sector.** Regulated businesses such as accountants and financial services need extra care and an approval step, which takes time.

## Is a freelancer cheaper than a social media agency?

For most small businesses, a freelancer is the better fit and the cheaper one. You talk directly to the person doing the work, so nothing gets lost in a hand-off, and you aren't paying for an office and three layers of account management.

An agency starts to make sense when you need a lot at once: paid ads, video production, influencer campaigns, PR. If that's you, great. If what you really need is a feed that looks consistent and posts that go out on time, a good freelancer will usually do it for less.

## What hidden costs should you ask about before you sign?

This is where cheap packages get expensive. Before you agree to anything, ask:

1. **How many revisions are included?** Two rounds per piece is common. Unlimited usually means slow.
2. **Is ad spend included?** Almost always not. Boosted posts and ads are paid to Meta or LinkedIn directly.
3. **Who pays for stock photos, fonts and tools?** Canva Pro, scheduling tools and paid images can add up.
4. **What's the notice period?** A rolling monthly agreement with 30 days' notice is fair. A 12-month lock-in for a small business is not.
5. **Who owns the files?** You should. Templates should be built in your own accounts so they stay with you if you part ways.
6. **Does the quote include VAT?** A freelancer who is registered for VAT adds 20% on top. Registration becomes compulsory when taxable turnover goes above [£90,000](https://www.gov.uk/vat-registration-thresholds), the figure on GOV.UK at the time of writing, so many small freelancers aren't registered, but it's worth asking so you compare like with like.

## What does good value actually look like?

The cheapest quote is rarely the best value, and the most expensive one isn't automatically the best work. What you're paying for:

- **Consistency.** Content that goes out when it's meant to, every week, without you chasing it.
- **Your brand, done properly.** Posts that look and sound like you, not like a template everyone else is using.
- **Your time back.** If you're spending your evenings in Canva, that's time you're not spending on the business.

One thing I'd be wary of: anyone who guarantees follower numbers, leads or sales. Results depend on your product, your prices, your market and your budget, and no honest freelancer controls all of those. What they can promise is good content, delivered on time.
