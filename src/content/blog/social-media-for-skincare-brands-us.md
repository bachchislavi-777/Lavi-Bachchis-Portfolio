<p class="tldr"><strong>The short answer:</strong> dependable skincare brand social media rests on three or four repeatable content pillars (product, education, proof and the people behind the brand), shared across Instagram, TikTok and Pinterest in a steady monthly mix. Keep captions to appearance and cleansing language, because US regulators look at what your content implies a product is for.</p>

Independent beauty brands rarely run short of products to photograph. What runs short is a plan, and the confidence to word things without wandering into claims the FDA or FTC would question. Here is how I would structure it, drawing on how I scope a new month of content for a skincare brand.

## What should a skincare brand post on social media?

Post a mix of four things: the product, how to use it, proof from real customers, and the people and process behind it. A feed made only of product shots feels like a catalog, and a feed made only of tips gives people no reason to buy. These are the content pillars I would start with:

| Pillar | What it is | Example formats |
|---|---|---|
| Product | Each item shown simply, with texture and packaging | Single photo, short texture clip, flat lay |
| Routine and education | How and when to use products, ingredient explainers | Carousel, Reel, Pinterest pin, a weekly series |
| Proof | Real customer words and photos, with permission | Customer repost, review quote, unboxing |
| Behind the brand | Founder, studio, packing day, sourcing | Photo dump, Stories, short talking-head video |

## What are good skincare Instagram content ideas?

Teaching posts and repeatable series are the easiest to keep going. Ideas that work for most independent brands:

- A "how to layer" carousel that moves from thinnest texture to richest.
- A weekly series with one fixed template, so it is easy to recognize and easy to produce.
- Ingredient focus posts in plain language: what it is, where it comes from, what it feels like.
- A "what's in the box" or packing-day video.
- A customer repost, with their written permission.
- A photo dump of the week in the studio, mixing polished and candid shots.
- Myth-versus-fact posts about routines (not about treating conditions).

Most of these double as beauty brand content ideas on TikTok and Pinterest. I show several in the [Solenne sample case study](../../work/solenne/). Solenne is a made-up small-batch skincare studio I created to show how I work, so the posts are sample work, not a real client's feed. It uses three pillars, a weekly education series and a grid planned so that no two neighbors share a format.

## How should you use Instagram, TikTok and Pinterest differently?

Use Instagram as your brand's home base, TikTok for short personable video and Pinterest for searchable, save-worthy images. Each platform rewards something slightly different, so adapt the same idea instead of pasting identical posts.

- **Instagram.** Carousels, Reels and Stories that show routines, products and the people behind them. Your bio and highlights act as a mini storefront.
- **TikTok.** Short videos with a clear first-second hook: a texture shot, a "stop doing this with your routine" tip, a packing session. Rougher and more personal usually works better than glossy.
- **Pinterest.** Vertical images with clear text overlays and descriptive titles, linked back to a blog post or product page. People use it to plan, so routine guides and ingredient explainers suit it.

You do not have to be everywhere. Start with one platform you can sustain and repurpose onto a second.

## What does a monthly mix look like?

A simple monthly plan assigns each week a job and reuses ideas across channels. This is a sample structure, not a quota:

| Week | Focus | Example output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Education | A how-to carousel, a Reel version of it, a Pinterest pin |
| 2 | Product | A launch or restock post, a texture clip, Stories with a link |
| 3 | Proof | A customer repost, a review quote, an FAQ Story |
| 4 | Behind the brand | A photo dump, a founder video, a preview of next month |

Add one email per month that pulls the best of the four weeks together. For the wider planning method, see [how I plan a month of social content](../social-media-content-plan-small-business/).

## What can you say in a skincare caption?

Describe how a product feels, what it is for in everyday terms and how people use it. Avoid wording that says it treats, cures, prevents or changes the way the body works.

The FDA's page [Is It a Cosmetic, a Drug, or Both?](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/laws-regulations/it-cosmetic-drug-or-both-or-it-soap) explains that a product's category depends on its intended use, which can be shown through label and advertising claims, consumer perception and ingredients. Cosmetics are described as products for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness or altering appearance. It names cleansers that are also intended to treat acne as products that can be regulated as drugs, lists claims to "regenerate cells" among drug claims, and defines drugs in part as products intended to affect the structure or any function of the body. Some products count as both, and then both sets of rules apply.

In practice, I would draft captions like this:

| Safer wording | Wording to avoid or run past a regulatory advisor |
|---|---|
| "Feels light and non-greasy" | "Treats acne" or "clears breakouts" |
| "Leaves skin looking smooth and hydrated" | "Boosts collagen production" |
| "A gentle cleanser for your evening routine" | "Repairs damaged skin cells" |

This is an editorial guide, not legal advice. Whether a particular claim is acceptable depends on the product, its ingredients and the full context, so check your own wording with a regulatory advisor. It also applies to your Stories, replies and the posts you repost. If a customer's comment says your product cleared their condition, think carefully before you share it.

Cosmetic businesses also have product-level obligations. The FDA's [MoCRA overview](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-laws-regulations/modernization-cosmetics-regulation-act-2022-mocra) covers the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act, including facility registration, product listing, safety substantiation records and adverse event reporting, with some exemptions for certain small businesses. That is a compliance question for you and your advisor, not a social media one, but it is worth knowing your content sits alongside it.

## How do you handle creators, gifted products and reviews?

Tell creators exactly how to disclose, and never fake or buy reviews. The FTC's [Endorsement Guides Q&A](https://consumer.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking) says free products can create a connection that should be disclosed where it is hard to miss, and that a company is ultimately responsible for what others do on its behalf. It expects advertisers to train and monitor the people promoting them, so tell creators which claims they can and cannot make and how to disclose, then check their posts. The FTC's [Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers) covers placement and wording.

The FTC's [Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers), effective October 21, 2024, prohibits fake reviews and testimonials, and knowingly buying fake indicators of social media influence, such as bot followers. Only repost real customer words, with permission.

## How do email and a blog fit in?

Use social for discovery and email for the relationship. A monthly newsletter and a small blog give your best social ideas a permanent home, and they make Pinterest pins and bio links more useful. A routine guide can become a blog post, a carousel, a pin and one newsletter section. Commercial email must follow the FTC's CAN-SPAM rules, including a clear opt-out and a valid postal address.
