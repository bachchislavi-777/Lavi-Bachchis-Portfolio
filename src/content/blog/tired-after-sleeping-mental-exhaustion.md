Ongoing stress, worry and mental load use real energy and can disturb the quality of your sleep. Rest that gives your mind a break, not just your body, usually helps. If you've felt this way for a few weeks without a clear reason, see your GP.</p>

There's a particular kind of tired that doesn't make sense. You went to bed on time. You slept through. And you still woke up feeling like you'd run a marathon in your sleep.

I've had weeks like that. At first I assumed I just needed more rest, so I tried earlier nights. It didn't help, because the tiredness wasn't really physical. It showed up in the weeks when my mind wouldn't switch off: planning, worrying, replaying conversations, running through tomorrow's to-do list at 11pm.

That's what this post is about. Sometimes we're not tired because we've done too much. We're tired because we've been carrying too much.

<p><em>A quick note before we start: I'm not a doctor or a therapist. This is general information to help you understand what might be going on, not medical advice. If you're worried about how you feel, please talk to your GP.</em></p>

## What is mental exhaustion?

Mental exhaustion is the drained, foggy, running-on-empty feeling that comes from long periods of mental or emotional strain, rather than physical effort.

It isn't a formal diagnosis. It's a way of describing what happens when your mind has been working hard for a long time without enough recovery: thinking, deciding, worrying, holding things together. You might also hear it called mental fatigue, or described as "running on empty".

The NHS lists stress, depression and big life challenges, such as bereavement or caring for a new baby, among the common psychological causes of tiredness. So if your tiredness feels more "in your head" than in your legs, you're not imagining it.

## Why you can sleep well and still feel exhausted

### Your mind has been working overtime

Thinking takes effort. Making decisions, solving problems, managing other people's needs and keeping track of everything you have to remember all draw on your energy, even if you've been sitting still all day.

When that mental load never really switches off, your mind doesn't get the recovery time it needs, however many hours you spend asleep.

### Stress keeps your body on alert

When you're stressed, your body releases hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol. In short bursts, that's useful: it's your body getting ready to deal with a challenge.

The problem is when the stress doesn't stop. Weeks of feeling on alert can leave you wired when you want to sleep and flat when you need to function. It can also make your sleep lighter or more broken, so you get the hours without the rest.

### Your rest isn't really rest

Scrolling your phone in bed, answering "just one more" email or lying awake planning tomorrow can all feel like resting. To your brain, though, they're more input.

Real rest is time when your mind isn't being asked to do anything: a walk without your phone, a few minutes of quiet, something absorbing that isn't work.

### Unfinished business follows you to bed

Our minds tend to hold on to unfinished tasks more than finished ones. That's why a single thing left undone can make a whole productive day feel incomplete, and why it can be hard to switch off at night. I've written more about this in [what the Zeigarnik effect is, and why unfinished tasks stay on your mind](../zeigarnik-effect-unfinished-tasks/).

## Common signs of mental exhaustion

Everyone's different, but these are some of the signs people often notice:

- **Feeling drained even after rest**, including after a weekend off or a full night's sleep
- **Brain fog**: forgetting things, losing your train of thought, reading the same paragraph three times
- **Finding decisions hard**, even small ones like what to have for dinner
- **A shorter fuse** than usual with the people around you
- **Feeling overwhelmed** by tasks you'd normally handle easily
- **Losing interest** in things you usually enjoy
- **Physical signs**, such as headaches, tense shoulders, changes in appetite, or skin flare-ups during stressful spells

Many of these signs overlap with other conditions, including depression, anxiety and physical conditions such as anaemia or thyroid problems. That's one reason it's worth getting checked if they don't ease.

## What can help

These are small, everyday things. They won't fix everything, but they can give your mind more room to recover.

1. **Take breaks that are actually breaks.** Five minutes away from screens, ideally outside, does more than five minutes of scrolling.
2. **Keep a steady sleep routine.** The NHS suggests going to bed and getting up at roughly the same times, winding down before bed and putting your phone away.
3. **Get the to-do list out of your head.** Write down what's unfinished and when you'll deal with it. Your brain is more likely to let go once there's a plan.
4. **Move a little.** It sounds backwards when you're tired, but gentle movement like a short walk often lifts energy more than resting on the sofa.
5. **Make one thing measurable.** On heavy days, tracking something small, like your steps or a glass of water with each meal, can give a foggy mind something steady to hold on to.
6. **Say it out loud.** Telling a friend, family member or colleague how you're feeling can lighten the load more than you'd expect.
7. **Look at what you're carrying.** If the same pressure keeps draining you, like workload, caring responsibilities or money worries, rest alone may not be enough. It might be time to ask for help with the source.

## When to see a GP

The NHS suggests booking a GP appointment if:

- you've been feeling tired for a few weeks and you're not sure why
- your tiredness is affecting your daily life
- you feel tired and have other symptoms, such as weight loss or changes in your mood

You can read more on the [NHS page on tiredness and fatigue](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tiredness-and-fatigue/), and find practical tips for stress and low mood on [NHS Every Mind Matters](https://www.nhs.uk/every-mind-matters/).

**If you're struggling right now**, you don't have to wait. Call your GP surgery, or call NHS 111 and choose the mental health option. If you're in immediate danger, call 999.

## A gentle reminder

Feeling exhausted doesn't mean you're weak, lazy or doing life wrong. It usually means you've been carrying a lot, for a long time, without enough chance to put it down.

Rest isn't a reward you earn once everything's done. It's a need.
