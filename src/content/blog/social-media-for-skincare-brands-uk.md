<p class="tldr"><strong>The short answer:</strong> a small skincare brand can build a steady feed on three content pillars (product, ritual and education, and proof from the studio), posted on one main platform with a second in a lighter role. Keep claims to what you can evidence, and never say a cosmetic treats a skin condition, because the ASA treats that as a medicine claim.</p>

Skincare is a crowded feed with more rules than most. The content that tends to suit independent brands, calm, useful and honest, is also the safest to publish.

This is marketing guidance, not legal advice. If a claim worries you, check it with your product safety assessor or a regulatory adviser before it goes live.

## What should a skincare brand post on social media?

Post a steady mix of product, education and proof, rather than a stream of product shots. When I built the [sample Solenne case study](../../work/solenne/), an imaginary small-batch botanical skincare studio in Bristol, I set the feed up around three pillars:

- **Product.** Each product shot simply, on its own colour, with one clear detail in the caption: what it is, how it feels, how it is made.
- **Ritual and education.** How-tos and a weekly series. In the sample, a recurring series called Ritual Notes uses one fixed template, so people recognise it at a glance.
- **Proof and studio.** Behind the scenes of a small batch, packing orders, and real customer words used with permission.

Education posts tend to be saved, and saves can help a post keep circulating. That is why "how to use face oil without the shine" beats a fourth photo of the same bottle.

## What are some skincare Instagram content ideas?

These are formats I'd rotate, each tied to a pillar:

1. **A how-to carousel.** Three or four steps, one idea per slide, a save prompt on the last.
2. **A short Reel with a text hook.** "Stop layering nine products" reads before the sound comes on.
3. **Ingredient focus.** What it is, where it comes from, what it does in the formula. Stay with what the ingredient does in the product, not what it will do for anyone's skin.
4. **Batch day.** Small-batch brands have a story big brands can't copy.
5. **A customer repost.** With permission, and with their words unedited.
6. **A photo dump.** Eight or nine casual frames from the month. Easy, human and quick to make.
7. **Founder Q&A.** Answer questions about the product, how it's made and how to use it.

Most of these also work as beauty brand content ideas on TikTok and as Pinterest pins. LinkedIn is worth a light touch only if you sell to salons, spas or retailers.

## What does a monthly content mix look like?

Plan a month at a time, with each pillar showing up every week. A reasonable starting point for four posts a week:

| Pillar | Share of posts | Example formats | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product | About 30% | Single image, product-on-colour, launch tile | One clear detail per caption, no big promises |
| Ritual and education | About 40% | Carousel, weekly series, Reel | Teach one thing per post |
| Proof and studio | About 20% | Batch day, customer repost, packing video | Real people, real words, with permission |
| Light and personal | About 10% | Photo dump, founder note, seasonal post | Keeps the feed from feeling like a catalogue |

These shares are a starting point I'd adjust against what your audience saves and replies to. They are not a formula, and they don't guarantee any particular result.

## How do you plan a skincare brand social media strategy in the UK?

Start with a month-ahead calendar rather than posting when you remember. The process I use for a new month is:

1. A short intro call to understand the brand and the products.
2. A brand kit, so colours, fonts and tone are set once.
3. A monthly calendar that you approve before anything is made.
4. Design, writing and scheduling, with two rounds of changes per piece.

For a UK skincare strategy, add an extra step: a claims check by whoever signs off your product information, before captions are scheduled. My general guide to [planning a content calendar](../social-media-content-plan-small-business/) covers the planning side in more depth.

## What can you say in a skincare caption?

You can describe how a product feels, how it is made and what it is for. You need evidence for anything about results. The Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) and the CAP Code apply to your social posts, and the ASA's [beauty and cosmetics guidance](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/beauty-and-cosmetics-general.html) says marketers must hold clinical evidence for efficacy claims. Cosmetics safety law is overseen by the Office for Product Safety and Standards (OPSS), and its [GOV.UK guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/making-cosmetic-products-available-to-consumers-in-great-britain) says the responsible person must be able to prove that claims made when marketing the product are correct.

Here is how I'd sort typical wording:

| Wording | Risk | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Two drops, pressed into damp skin" | Low | Describes use, makes no promise |
| "Skin feels softer" | Lower | A subjective sensory claim; the ASA calls these easier to substantiate |
| "Made in batches of 200 in Bristol" | Low | A fact you can show |
| "Clinically proven to reduce dark spots" | Higher | An objective efficacy claim that needs proper human evidence |
| "Visible results in 7 days" | Higher | A cumulative claim that needs a high level of evidence |
| "Treats acne" or "calms eczema" | Do not use | A medicinal claim |
| Retouched before-and-after photos | Do not use | The ASA has upheld rulings on retouching that exaggerates results |

Treat the table as a prompt for questions, not a clearance. Whether a specific claim is acceptable depends on the product, the evidence and the wording around it.

### Why is "treats acne" a problem for a cosmetic?

Because a claim to treat or prevent a skin condition is a medicinal claim, whatever the product is called. The ASA's [guidance on acne and spots](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/health-acne-and-spots.html) says claims to treat acne or spots are likely to be seen as medicinal, and a product that needs a medicines licence must have one before it can be marketed. The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) decides whether a product is a medicine. Its [guidance on what counts as a medicinal product](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68cad9711eabc899da7084f1/MHRA_GN8.pdf) looks at claims made anywhere, including websites, testimonials and social media, and names acne, eczema, dermatitis and psoriasis as medical conditions. So the simplest rule for a social calendar is to talk about the product, not the condition, and to think twice before reposting a customer comment that says a product cleared one.

## Do you have to label influencer posts about skincare?

Yes, if there is a payment, a free product or any other incentive. The ASA's [guidance on recognising ads on social media](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recognising-ads-social-media.html) covers everything from a post about a gifted product to a paid collaboration, and treats a post with an affiliate link or code as an ad. It says a label such as "Ad" should appear upfront, not only after "See more". Labels such as "Gifted" or "In association with" are unlikely to be clear enough, it advises against "sponsored", and a brand tag, a discount code or a note in a bio is not enough on its own. Both brands and influencers are responsible, so put the labelling rule in your creator brief.

## Are fake reviews banned for skincare brands?

Yes. Under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, fake reviews and concealed incentivised reviews have been banned since 6 April 2025. The Competition and Markets Authority's [fake reviews guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fake-reviews-cma208) explains what businesses now need to do. In practice: only use words from real customers, don't cherry-pick in a way that misleads, and say so when a review was rewarded. In my sample work, any review tile is labelled as a sample.

## How do a newsletter and journal fit in?

Your newsletter and journal give you somewhere to say more than a caption allows, and they belong to you rather than to an algorithm. A simple pattern is to publish one journal post a month, such as a ritual explained or an ingredient story, then turn it into a carousel and a newsletter. Social drives readers to the journal; the newsletter brings them back. Email has its own rules on permission, so make sure everyone on your list has opted in.
